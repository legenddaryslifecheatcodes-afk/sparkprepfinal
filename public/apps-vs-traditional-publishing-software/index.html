Why an A.P.P.S. is different from traditional publishing software

By Joshua Davis, founder of Legenddary SparkPrep · Published November 18, 2026

Short answer: Traditional publishing software does one job at a time: format, check, prepare for print or design a cover. An Automated Publishing Production System (A.P.P.S.) does all of those jobs in one system, fixes problems for you and double-checks every fix, so your book files are ready before the printer sees them.

The old way: one tool for every job

If you are publishing your first book, here is what usually happens. You write in Word. You make a cover in a design app or pay a designer. You format the pages in another program or pay a formatter. Then you upload to Amazon KDP or IngramSpark and wait.

If something is wrong, the printer sends your book back with words you may never have heard: bleed, safety area, ink coverage, PDF/X-1a. Now you have to work out which tool caused it, fix it there and try again. Each tool only knows its own job. None of them looks at the whole book the way the printer does. You end up being the person who holds it all together.

That is how SparkPrep started. Founder Joshua Davis's own book, Legenddary Mindset: The Awakening, was sent back 18 times.

What traditional tools are good at, and where they stop

  • Formatters lay out your pages. Most don't check your cover or your printer's ink rules.
  • Preflight checkers tell you what is wrong. They usually don't fix it.
  • Prepress tools (like Adobe Acrobat Pro) can fix print problems, but they are made for professionals and take skill to use safely.
  • Cover design apps make beautiful covers, but a cover can look perfect on screen and still have no bleed or the wrong spine width.
  • Freelancers can do it all for you, at a price. Reedsy reports a median of about $930 for a professional cover and an average of about $800 for interior formatting, and it often takes several weeks.

None of these are bad. They just each do one piece, and the gaps between them are where books get rejected.

The A.P.P.S. way: one system that works together

An Automated Publishing Production System (A.P.P.S.) brings the pieces together. It helps you make the book, checks it, fixes it and checks the fix, in one place, so nothing gets lost between tools.

Why it matters for a first-time author

  • You don't have to learn printer language first. The system knows KDP's and IngramSpark's rules and explains each problem in plain English.
  • Fewer rejections. Problems are caught before upload, not after. KDP says a book, or each fix, takes about 3 business days to go live, so every rejection costs you days.
  • One place to go back to. Change your page count and the spine changes too. In an A.P.P.S. the cover and pages live together, so it can tell you.
  • Less money spent on rounds of changes. You fix your own book, on your own schedule.

What an A.P.P.S. doesn't do

It won't write your book for you, and it can't promise the printer will accept it: KDP and IngramSpark always make the final call. What it does is make sure your files follow their published rules before you upload, and tell you honestly when something, like a picture that's too small, needs new art from you.

Common questions

How is an A.P.P.S. different from a book formatter?

A book formatter lays out your pages. An Automated Publishing Production System (A.P.P.S.) also designs the cover, checks both files against the printer's rules, fixes the problems it finds and double-checks every fix, all in one system.

Is an A.P.P.S. the same as a preflight checker?

No. A preflight checker only tells you what is wrong. An A.P.P.S. includes a preflight check, then fixes what it safely can, checks the fixed file again and gives you print-ready files.
