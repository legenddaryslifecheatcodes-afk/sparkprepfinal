How self-publishing got here

Every big step in self-publishing let one more kind of person put a book into the world. Here is the short version:

  1. Desktop publishing (1985): programs like Aldus PageMaker moved page layout from the print shop to a home computer. You could design a book, but you still had to pay a printer for a big stack of copies.
  2. Print on demand (late 1990s): Ingram's Lightning Source started printing one book at a time, when someone ordered it. No warehouse, no boxes in the garage.
  3. Publish it yourself online (2007 onward): Amazon's Kindle Direct Publishing (KDP) opened in 2007, and IngramSpark opened to independent authors in 2013. Anyone could upload a book for free and sell it worldwide.
  4. A tool for every job: as more people published, separate apps appeared for formatting pages, designing covers and checking files (preflight). Each one does one job well.
  5. The A.P.P.S. (2026): those jobs come together in one Automated Publishing Production System. That is the step SparkPrep takes.

It worked. Bowker counted about 3.53 million self-published titles in 2025. More people are publishing than ever.

The problem the last step left behind

Uploading got easy. Getting the file right did not. KDP and IngramSpark still check your cover and pages against strict print rules: the exact cover size, the spine width, bleed, safe margins, picture sharpness, fonts and ink. A file that looks perfect on your screen can still be sent back.

So a first-time author ends up doing a job no one told them about. You move the book between a writing app, a cover app, a formatter and maybe a checker, and you hope nothing breaks between them. Or you hire freelancers: Reedsy reports a median of about $930 for a cover and an average of about $800 for interior formatting.

That is how SparkPrep started. Founder Joshua Davis's own book, Legenddary Mindset: The Awakening, was sent back by the printer 18 times. He built SparkPrep so no author has to go through that again.
